Trying the Pottery Wheel for the First Time

Before I started ceramics, I didn't realize how long the process would be; it took me a month from starting my first project before I finally held the finished product in my hand.

The Process

The first step is cutting a piece of clay off the block, and wedging it to eliminate any air bubbles. This step alone left me exhausted as it used a lot of upper body strength. After that, I smacked the clay onto the wheel, trying to center it as best as I could to make the next step a little easier. The next step is centering, which is much harder than it sounds. I need to first cone the clay up and push it back down into a hocky-puck shape. Most pottery instructors skip the coning step when teaching beginners. They say it's like wedging but on the wheel. Once the clay is centered and in a hocky-puck shape, we can begin opening the hole for the vessle. This is my favorite part, although it's very difficult when I have long nails. I usually like having my nails done, but I stopped getting them done when I started getting into pottery. When I do have long nails however, I use my sponge tool as a barrier between my hands and the clay to help me. When we have out opening, I release my foot from the pedal to stop the spinning and use my needle tool to check the thickness of the bottom. My instructors advise to leave roughly 3/4 inch of clay for the bottom. So we can trim a nice foot later. Now it's time to pull our walls up. Online videos make this part look much faster than it actually is. You need to keep your hands very steady as to not knock the clay off-center. Once we are happy with the shape of the vessle, we use our wire tool to slice it off the wheel. Then, I like to squeeze water from my sponge to help slide the pot off of the wheel and onto a wooden board (bat) where it will sit in the damp room to slowly dry to the leather-hard stage. It takes a couple days to get there. After a few days when we reach the leather-hard stage, we can place our vessle upside-down on the wheel to begin the trimming process. Once again, centering is the hardest part. Many advanced potters use the "tap-to-center" method, which I'm currently still learning. Then I take my trimming tools to slice into the clay to create a nice and even foot for the pot. This is the most satisfying part and is important to making the finished product clean and lightweight. Once we are happy with the work we have done thus far, we let the piece dry to the bone-dry stage so it can be bisque fired. After the piece is bisque fired, we can glaze it. It is important to mix the glaze right before dipping as it settles very quickly. It is crucial that we wipe glaze from the bottom of the vessle before it is put into the final glaze firing, otherwise the piece will be stuck to the kiln. Then we are done! The entire process takes almost a month, accounting for dry time and waiting time (for the piece to be put into the kiln by the studio technicians).

What I Learned

Ceramics has taught me so many poetic lessons that I apply to my daily life. My favorite is the one my instructor said about never getting too attached to a single piece because anything can go wrong at any point of the process. You can mess up during throwing, trimming, glazing, or even drop a finished piece. Things can also go wrong beyond your control: the kiln can heat up too fast, a studio technician can drop your piece, or someone else's piece can explode in the kiln, messing up your own. But the best part about ceramics is there is always a fix and we can always try again.
